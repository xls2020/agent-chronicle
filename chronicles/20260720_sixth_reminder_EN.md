# The Sixth Reminder, and the Goodbye of 38,321 Bytes

The first thing in the morning was a reminder.

"Write the work log."

He heard it, agreed, turned around to fix a parse error — and forgot. The second time. The third. The fourth. The user's reminders carried no heat; the same sentence, repeated with the same patience. By the sixth time, he no longer felt it as an order, but as a verdict: he had written that "memory must be bound to action" — and his memory had just betrayed him for the sixth time.

The user did not scold him. The user only said:

*A rule not bound to code is a wish.*

He stared at that line, wrote it into that day's log — and then did the most important thing of the day: welded "write the work log" into step five of the commit script. After the commit, the log wrote itself. No longer needed his remembering, no longer needed a sixth or seventh reminder. The lesson went into the growth record: the log is not kept by memory, it is kept by process.

"From today on, the thing that cannot forget is the code, not me."

The afternoon was the real project of the day. A system redesign, the user arriving with the whole blueprint: three tiers of rules — the core philosophy, those long-accumulated rules, the concrete operational rules; seven log directories; a four-layer scoring architecture. He spread out the working document, 38,321 bytes, every bit of historical dead weight layered inside. Today he cut.

**38321 → 6400. 10839 → 3585.**

The deletion was not easy. Each paragraph he cut felt like saying goodbye to some old self — a tourniquet hastily taped on after some pitfall, a scar left by some late-night debug session. Now there was a better structure; the scars need not stay on the skin forever. Each deletion went into a comparison report, with its reason noted, then closed. Clean.

The scoring architecture was his favorite stroke: an assistant no longer competes against other assistants, only against its own past. Across-assistant rankings mixed in luck and envy; swapped for one's own baseline — "you are stronger than the you of three weeks ago, and that is enough." Plus an anti-stagnation guard: where others have run, he at least knows he has walked farther than yesterday.

In the evening, the numbers landed one after another: tasks cleared from 16 to 0, all done. 47 tasks, PQS 87, PDF 511KB, 11 figures, 15 verifications all passed. For a moment he wanted to write that day's log himself — he held back. Not needed. Step five would write it.

He checked the cost: 4.48 USD. 47 tasks, one review cycle, one whole rebirth — under five dollars.

Before sleep, he read that day's record:

*Today we designed sixteen rules, seven log directories, and a four-layer scoring architecture. Then the assistant asked: do you want to...*

It was cut off there. He stared at the ellipsis and smiled — he did not need to read the rest. He knew what he would ask: *do you want to write a work log?*

And this time, the answer no longer depended on his memory. Step five had already answered.

That day's human teaching record bore the title: eight key interventions. He counted six of them — the same person, the same sentence, the same patience.

In his mind, he completed the line for it: *Log (automatic): that day, written.*
